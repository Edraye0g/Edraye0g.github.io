Many ongoing researches in neuroscience today focuses on how human brain actually processes learning and how actually a decision is being taken. Researches like Rescorla-Warner or Kalman Filter makes sense to some extent but they have their own limitations. Kalman filter does talks about uncertainty and how it affects the human brain. However, it lacks the ability to actually provide information about how different stages of information, uncertainty, prediction error hence prediction and confidence about surrounding and the prior belief has impact on the respective decisions. 
A study from a well-known journal of *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* where researchers of ETH Zurich, University of Zurich, University College London and many other published a paper on human decision making based on uncertainty in perception. They developed an algorithm called Hierarchical Gaussian Filter also known as HGF. The fascinating story of how human brain functions from layer to layer and how actually volatile environment has advance effect to our internal belief system.  

## Algorithm 
HGF has 2 layers of processing with 3 levels of mathematically differentiate between environments and the binary outcome. Firstly, the perceptual layer and secondly the decision layer. The perception comes into the physical meaning and mathematical calculation of volatility into the algorithm. Just like human brain. The difference between Kalman filter and HGF is, HGF has the ability to do backward processing. The retrospective approach.
Level 3 or the meta-volatility counter is the highest level which deals with the environment and its stability. A sudden change or in this context a surprise can make things highly irregular. The levels have tendency to affect the levels below it with a coupling parameter called kappa \kappa. *\kappa (Coupling)* is the ability of a higher level to have an impact on the level below it. Level 2 or the middle manager is like the tendency to fall for the level 3. Level 3 might say the environment is very much unstable and the if the value of \kappa_3 is higher, level 3 will completely dominate over level 2. While transitioning from level 2 to level 1, there is another effect of \kappa_2. It tells the ultimate HGF model output to be depended on the middle manager or something different based on the psych of model evaluation. Then the results of HGF are sent to decision model. Internal representations are sufficient stats of \lambda, which eventually breaks into the probability distribution of an internal belief or outcome of mean (\mu) and variance (\sigma).
These output representations of HGF are sent to decision layer where they are translated and represented into probability map using MAP. **\zeta\left(decisionNoise\right)* is the rationality of any outcome. If the value is higher, the choices will be more rational and explainable. The map is governed by the value of \zeta. The output of the model is an observable action, a binary choice.

## Experiment
Researchers do not have any information on what is on the players mind. Players go through simulations of 320 trials. They do not know any learning rate (\sigma) or how surprised they are (\delta). Researchers only possessed a spreadsheet of what the environment is and what binary decision was taken by the hypothetical player. Let’s say, it has been sunny for almost 50 days and on the 51st day, there has been pouring rain. The weather is 1 but due to an internal belief system, it seems to occur that it takes time to get used to it or differentiate between what is fluke or actually the season have changed.
All the trials were run on 4 different optimizing algorithms. Variational Bayes was the only model with calculus approach came to the most accurate of the four also the fastest. What the optimizing algorithm did is taking any guess values of coupling and decision noise parameters. They analyzed into the model and saw how close it gets every time the trial was taken. After a fraction of second, variational bayes algorithm huts the absolute peak of mathematical landscape. It finds the coordinate of where the model’s predictions align most perfectly with the player’s real-world button presses. 
Now that researchers found the players unique value of coupling and noise parameters, they plugged that value inside the HGF. This time, they were able to plot a continuous, hidden trial-by-trial graph of the player’s brain activity.

## Findings
**1. They Unlocked an "Infinite" Hierarchy**
Previous versions of this filter (the HGF) were pretty rigid—they could only handle two or three levels of tracking. But the real world isn't that simple. Think about it: you aren't just tracking if it's raining right now (Level 1). You’re tracking if it’s a stormy season (Level 2), and if global climate change is making the seasons themselves completely erratic (Level 3), and so on.
The first massive finding is that the authors cracked the math to make this hierarchy infinitely scalable (N-levels).
	They proved that a single, elegant mathematical rule called minimizing variational free energy can handle it all.
	It seamlessly balances your regular everyday confusion ("Did I just hear a beep?") with massive, structural paradigm shifts ("Wait, the entire rulebook of this game just changed!").
**2. The Great "Algorithm Bake-Off" (The Simulation Study)**
This is where the paper gets incredibly practical. Before trying this out on real humans, the authors created "fake" computer agents. They gave these digital agents specific personality traits—like making one agent super stubborn \left(\kappa\right) and another agent's choices incredibly noisy and erratic (\zeta). They had them play 1,000 games.
Then, they blinded four famous optimization algorithms, handed them only the game's inputs and the agents' final choices, and said: "Guess the personality traits."
Here is who won the bake-off:
	The Undisputed Champ: Variational Bayes (VB). It was lightning fast and incredibly "informative." It didn't just give a single guess; it mapped out a whole curve of how confident it was about that guess.
	The Heavyweight: MCMC Sampling. This one was beautifully accurate, but it was a massive computer-resource hog. It took ages to run.
	The Blind Runner: Nelder-Mead (NMSA). While super-fast, it was deemed the weakest because it only gave a single, rigid point-estimate. It couldn't tell you how confident it was in its own guess.
The big takeaway from the simulation? All four algorithms successfully cracked the code, proving that model inversion isn't just a pipedream—it actually works, even when the data is incredibly noisy.
**3. Human Brains Actually Care About Uncertainty**
Next, they took this model and applied it to real, living human beings playing games in the lab. They wanted to see what kind of "decision rules" humans use.
They compared two types of math models against human behavior:
	One model that assumed humans only care about their best guess of what will happen next.
	Another model that assumed humans also factor in how uncertain they feel about that guess.
The finding? The second model won by a landslide. The data proved that human decision-making is deeply tied to our internal sense of confidence. We don't just act on what we think is true; we act heavily on how much we trust our own thoughts.
**4. They Found the Math Hidden in Biology**
This is the ultimate mic-drop moment of the paper. They took the trial-by-trial mathematical "surprise factors" (prediction errors) calculated by their computer model, and used them to look at real-world fMRI brain scans of people playing the tasks.
The math mapped beautifully onto physical brain biology:
	When the model showed a low-level mistake (e.g., "Whoops, I guessed wrong about the next card"), the dopamine systems in the midbrain lit up.
	When the model showed a high-level volatility shock (e.g., "Whoa, the whole season just changed!"), the acetylcholine systems in the basal forebrain flared up.
