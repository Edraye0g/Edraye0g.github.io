First day at college and you are asked to introduce yourself in front of the class. You told something completely different. Everyone laughed at you. You felt disconnected and demotivated. After some time, another classmate of yours were laughed at. You felt at bad but also at ease. It happened because you have empathy. But the question is why did you feel at ease? You were not alone in that room being laughed at. Human brain constantly searches for connection, familiarity and similarity. 
It’s called minimal group effect according to *Henri Tajfel.* Even a simple familiarity with another individual can trigger this effect. This is social categorization and we can see the effects of in every day life. From economical to political to cultural to racial, people are grouped into many things based on belief, understanding, color and attire. We feel connected with the people we find similarities. On the other hand, some extent of prejudice and hostility are being shown to the individual who are not in our group or even related to it. A study from University of Zurich [Asymmetric cognitive learning mechanisms underlying the persistence of intergroup bias](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-024-00061-0) says that human have prior belief about **ingroup vs outgroup** or **us vs them** mechanism inbuilt in them when they are categorized intentionally. The study first talked about how contact theory failed to explain the phenomena of intergroup bias. If people of different group are pushed in to a society to operate, they will somehow fight for their beliefs due to competition and unsupervised condition. The study proved that, even there’s a small percentage of people bring benefit to other group, the perception only changes for the small percentage but not as a whole group while overlooking the anomalies in the ingroup if they bring disturbance. This theory can explain why terrorism is pushed only to a certain religion after 9/11 while same incident done by someone from their group/culture lead to no reaction from the people. 

## The Star-Harvest Game
To observe these biases in real-time, researchers utilized the star-harvest game, a multiplayer grid-based task.
 **Game Mechanics**
- **Objective:** Five players move across a 2D grid to collect stars for points.
- **Conflict Mechanism ("Zapping"):** Players can sacrifice a move to "zap" another player, sending them to a "time-out zone" for three turns.
- **Bot Behavior:** Participants played with "bot-players" programmed with two distinct behaviors: **Zappers-** Highly competitive; they zap any player standing between them and a star. **Avoiders-** Cooperative/Non-competitive; they move away to find other stars if the path is blocked.
The study employed six conditions to cross-reference behavior and identity:
1. **Grouped Conditions:** Participants chose a team color and played with two ingroup members (same color) and two outgroup members (different color).
2. **Neutral Conditions:** Participants played with four players of different colors with no team affiliation.
3. **Homogeneous vs. Heterogeneous:** Bots were either all the same type (all zappers or all avoiders) or a mix of both types.

## Findings
Intergroup bias is actually a neurobiological thing. One can only move from it based on more learning and changing attributes. Perception changes with social interaction and knowledge. The study showed that, human has a type of asymmetric learning while updating their belief about us vs them context. 
![]()

The study utilized a PLA (Prior, Learning, Attribution) Model to identify how participants updated their beliefs about other players.
**Mechanism I: Biased Priors**
Participants entered the game with a **prior** bias. Even in **all-avoider** conditions where no one ever zapped, participants were significantly more likely to zap outgroup members. This indicates a baseline expectation of outgroup hostility that exists independent of evidence.
**Mechanism II: Asymmetric Learning Rates**
Learning is defined as the rate at which a participant updates their belief about a player’s likelihood to zap based on observed actions.
- Negative Learning: Participants learned about the **zapping** behavior of outgroups more readily than they did for ingroups.
- Positive Learning: Learning rates for **avoidance** (cooperation) were generally lower than for zapping, but they were particularly stunted for outgroup members.
**Mechanism III: Biased Group-Level Attribution**
This mechanism involves generalizing the behavior of one individual to other members of their group.
- Negative Generalization: If one outgroup member zapped, participants quickly increased their **zap expectations** for the other outgroup member.
- Positive Localization: When an outgroup member behaved cooperatively (avoiding zapping), participants were less likely to attribute this positive trait to the rest of the outgroup.
- Ingroup Symmetry: For ingroups, attribution was more symmetrical; both positive and negative behaviors were handled with more flexibility.

The study’s findings were supported by several behavioral metrics beyond simple zapping rates:
**Zapping and Path-Crossing**
Metric | Ingroup Observation | Outgroup Observation
Zap Rate	| Significantly lower, even if the ingroup member was a zapper. | Significantly higher, even if the outgroup member was an "avoider."
Path-Crossing | Participants were more likely to move into a vulnerable position near an ingroup member. | Participants avoided sharing a row/column with outgroup members, fearing a zap.

**Post-Game Assessments**
Star Allocation: After the game, participants were asked to distribute ten extra stars. They consistently favored ingroup members, even those who had zapped them, while depriving outgroup members of stars.
**Intentionality Ratings:**
Ingroup Zapping: Often interpreted as "selfish" (trying to get stars) rather than "harmful."
Outgroup Zapping: Frequently interpreted as having "harmful intent" (wanting to prevent others from succeeding).

**Model Fit Comparison**
The research compared three computational models to determine which best explained participant behavior:

Model | Description | WAIC (Lower is Better)
P Model | Prior effects only | 4251.06
PL Model | Prior + Learning mechanisms | 4211.63
PLA Model | Prior + Learning + Group Attribution | 4206.67
The PLA Model was the most accurate, confirming that all three asymmetric mechanisms contribute to the persistence of bias.

Interactions are not a panacea. Simple contact is insufficient if the underlying cognitive processing remains asymmetric. In competitive settings, outgroups are quickly categorized with "low warmth" stereotypes, which are then reinforced by faster learning of negative actions. To reduce bias, it is not enough for outgroups to behave well; the cognitive mechanism that "localizes" that good behavior to a single individual must be addressed to allow for group-level re-evaluation.
