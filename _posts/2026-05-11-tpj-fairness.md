Making decisions at an organizational level gets harder with each variable of consideration. Efficiency, time, money, distribution, fairness and many other things. Considering a psychological evaluation on Humean’s utilitarianism and Rawlsian Fairness mechanism, a study from *PNAS* journal shows the how human mind think on the basis of social welfare on an individual level vs group. The study [*Tipping the balance between fairness and efficiency through temporoparietal stimulation*](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2409395121) showed how a specific region called temporoparietal junction called on fairness for worst-off individual. 
Human mind has 9 levels of cognition such as clarifying right and wrong, understanding social rule, resource awareness etc. While distributing resources among individual, a person seems to be thinking about 3 tug and war way. Maximum efficiency on the distributed resources, fairness among left off individual and self interest of greed and corruption. In the study, self interest was ignored or taken out of consideration to only find the decision making between groups vs individual. 
The utilitarian efficiency is about the greater good. Allocating the most amounts of resources to the group. It follows the rule of equality rather than equity. On the contrary, there is Rawlsian fairness. If among A and B, B is the worst-off in allocation, the resources will be again divided to prioritize B. The fairness system does not care about maximum allocation. Rather it serves equity among the society or the people in need. 
The experiment used interesting observation game among individuals to understand which brain area lights up while taking decision siding with fairness and verified by inhibiting response to the specific area. This is where the main task lies. What happens while inhibiting TPJ? How efficiency and fairness jump between the cliff of allocation and social welfare?

## Experiment
-	**Preference Learning Task:** Before the main trials, participants learned the subjective food preferences of two "agents" (e.g., Agent A might value smarties more than peanuts) by observing their choices. 
-	**Welfare Decision Task:** Participants chose between two different distributions of food snacks for these agents. The distributions were designed so that one option might be more efficient (higher total value) while the other was fairer (higher value for the agent currently receiving less). 
-	**Experiment 1 (fMRI):** 43 participants performed the task in an MRI scanner to correlate brain activity with specific decision rules. 
-	**Experiment 2 (tDCS):** 50 participants performed the task while undergoing transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS). This experiment used three conditions: Anodal (excitatory), Cathodal (inhibitory), and Sham (control) to test for causal links. 
-	**Perspective-Taking Task:** Participants also completed a task where they had to judge a scene from their own perspective versus that of an avatar to measure their perspective-taking capacity.

## Computational Framework
-	Charness-Rabin Model: The researchers used this "quasi-maximin" model to quantify the balance between efficiency and fairness using a parameter (denoted as δ). A higher δ indicates a stronger reliance on the Rawlsian maximin principle. 
-	General Linear Mixed Models (GLMMs): These were used to assess how well different criteria (Rawlsian, efficiency, Nash product, inequality aversion) predicted participant choices. 
-	Mediation Analysis: A formal mediation analysis was performed to see if changes in perspective-taking skills could explain the changes in fairness preferences.


## Findings
-	**TPJ Activation:** The fMRI results showed significantly higher activation in the right TPJ when participants made choices based on Rawlsian fairness compared to efficiency or inequality aversion.
-	**Causal Influence of tDCS:** Reducing TPJ excitability with cathodal stimulation significantly reduced the weight participants gave to Rawlsian fairness. This effect was specific to fairness; the stimulation did not affect efficiency or inequality concerns. 
-	**Role of Perspective-Taking:** The reduction in fairness was statistically mediated by a reduction in perspective-taking ability. Essentially, when the TPJ was inhibited, participants found it harder to "step into the shoes" of the worst-off person, leading them to favor efficiency instead.

Neurobiological Mechanism: The TPJ serves as a neural mechanism that allows us to balance conflicting moral norms by enabling us to focus on the needs of individuals rather than just group averages. Recasting the TPJ: These findings suggest the TPJ is not just about "self vs. others" but plays a more nuanced role in adjudicating between different types of prosocial behavior (e.g., being efficient vs. being fair). Developmental and Clinical Insights: The study links the maturation of fairness in children to the development of perspective-taking skills and suggests that certain social disorders (like Borderline Personality Disorder) characterized by TPJ hypoactivation may lead to altered social decision-making.

## Thoughts
Firstly, the experiment was done avoiding the conflict of self-interest which can rise the chances of biasness and greed. Secondly, the whole experiment only saw compared between smarties/peanuts. What might happen if there was any life and death situation? This is such a bigger question. The perspective-taking task only showed how a person leaned on others perspective and thought like the avatar in cathodal stimulation. This is a partially explained feature. Other features like empathy and logic can also play significant role.
