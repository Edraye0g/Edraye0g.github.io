**Probability** and **Uncertainty** are almost identical to listen but have distinction between them. Probability means a likelihood of an outcome. There’s an 80 percent chance of cyclone in a coastal area. That is a high probable cause. What if the percentage is 40? This raises uncertainty. So, we can say uncertainty is just probability but with less percents right? No. 

Probability is just a formal way of quantifying uncertainty. However, in cognition science, we use risk as uncertainty with known probabilities and true uncertainty where probability itself is unknown and ill-defined. Probability can capture uncertainty with informational advantage. But, what if there’s gap in information? A drug trial is uncertain. There’s least amount of prior data to be stood upon. So, uncertainty refers to the confidence of the likelihood. Less confidence means more uncertainty. In neural level, probability shows how often specific region in the human brain fires. On the other side, uncertainty means how constantly that specific region fires. 

![](/assests/val3.png)

Human brain process probability and uncertainty differently. There are other factors come along while talking about probability which are valanced and generic probability. Valenced probability is that which have emotional attachment with human judgment. An exam result can be good or bad, but the emotional connection is deep (connecting reward and punishment). However, traditional studies to understand the probability only comprised with valenced or motivational outcome. But, a new study from the *Journal of Neuroscience* showed that the effect of probability and uncertainty in human brain deeply affected by both the valenced and generic outcome. The paper [*Neural Representation of Valenced and Generic Probability and Uncertainty*](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/44/30/e0195242024) showed that uncertainty put weight to amplify any emotional drive during valenced probability while generic probability worked as a baseline with lesser effect and more numerical outcome to human with less interest to biological output during avoidance. It used action vs attention where action is the response time towards visual cue and attention is the metric of pupil dilation during motivational and neutral probabilities.

## Experiment
**Phase 1: The Balancing Act (Subjective Value Matching)**
The researchers didn't want to just compare "sugar" to "bitterness"; they needed to ensure that the participant’s desire for the sweet drink was exactly as strong as their aversion to the bitter drink.
-	**Selection:** They screened 72 participants to find people who had balanced emotional reactions.
- **The "Price" of Effort:** Participants performed a Becker-DeGroot-Marschak (BDM) auction. Instead of paying money, they "bid" physical effort (hand-grip strength). 
*The Logic:* If you’re willing to squeeze a gripper at 50% of your maximum strength to get a sweet drop, and 50% to avoid a bitter drop, those two liquids are now mathematically "balanced" in your mind.
- The Filter: 22 people were sent home because they couldn't find a pair of liquids that the participant liked and hated equally. This left 50 people with perfectly matched subjective values.

![](/assests/val2.png)

**Phase 2: The Training (Pavlovian Conditioning)**
Before the brain scan, participants had to learn a new visual language. They were taught to associate seven visual cues (different patterns of colored squares) with specific outcomes.
-	The Probabilities: Each cue represented a different chance (p) of getting a liquid:
*p=1.0*: Guaranteed outcome (Sweet, Bitter, or Neutral).
*p=0.5*: A coin flip (Sweet, Bitter, or Neutral).
*p=0*: Guaranteed nothing (The control/baseline).
-	The Mastery Test: Participants had to achieve 80% accuracy in identifying what each cue meant before they were allowed into the scanner. This ensured that during the fMRI, their brains were actually making predictions, not just guessing

**Phase 3: The Main Event (fMRI & Trial Architecture)**
Once in the scanner, participants went through multiple blocks of trials. Each trial followed a strict, timed sequence to separate the brain's *thinking* phase from its *tasting* phase.
**The Anatomy of a Single Trial:**
-	**The Cue (1.5s):** The visual pattern appears. The brain starts predicting.
-	**The Prediction/Rating:** Participants either predict the outcome or rate the pleasantness of the cue.
-	**The Delay (1.1–4.2s):** A "jittered" wait period. This is crucial for fMRI—it allows the blood-oxygen signal from the cue to settle before the liquid arrives, preventing the two signals from blurring together.
-	**The Outcome:** The fixation cross turns blue, and the participant receives the liquid drop (or nothing) based on the cue’s probability.
-	**The Rating & Rinse:** The participant rates how the liquid actually tasted, then swallows and rinses with a neutral liquid to "reset" the tongue for the next round.

**To get a full picture of the "Decision-Making" state, the researchers collected:**
-	fMRI Data: Brain activity (focused on mPFC, Insula, and Occipital cortex).
-	Pupil Dilation: As a proxy for arousal and Expected Uncertainty.
-	Response Times: To measure motivational "urge" (how fast they clicked).
-	Heart Rate & Respiration: To track the body's physiological stress response to the bitter liquid.

## Findings
**The Decomposition of Choice Variables**
In classical decision theory (like Expected Utility Theory), a decision is usually calculated as Value times Probability. This paper proves that the brain doesn't just calculate the final product; it keeps the variables separate. The brain maintains "Generic" representations of probability and uncertainty that are independent of the reward. This suggests that decision-making isn't just about seeking pleasure; it's about maintaining an accurate internal "map" of environmental statistics.

**Functional Dissociation in the mPFC**
The medial Prefrontal Cortex (mPFC) is often called the "CEO" of decision-making. This study adds a layer of sophistication to that title:
-	Anterior (Front) mPFC: Acts as an Information Processor. It tracks the probability of any outcome (Generic Probability).
-	Posterior (Back) mPFC: Acts as a Value Evaluator. It tracks the probability of meaningful outcomes (Valenced Probability).
This anatomical split explains why some brain injuries might leave a person able to understand the odds of a gamble but unable to feel the weight of the potential win or loss.

**Expected Uncertainty" vs. Risk Aversion**
Most decision-making literature treats uncertainty as a bug that humans try to avoid (risk aversion). This paper offers a different perspective:
-	Expected Uncertainty: Since participants learned the cues perfectly, the 50/50 chance wasn't a surprise. It was a known state of the world.
-	The Pupil Signal: The fact that pupils tracked "precision" (the inverse of uncertainty) suggests that in a stable environment, the brain prioritizes certainty as a form of Generic Attention. It shows the brain is tuned to seek out high-precision information to guide future behavior.

**The Insula as a Salience Filter**
In decision-making, "salience" is what grabs your attention. This paper redefines the Insula’s role. It ignored the neutral (water) cues, even when they were high-probability. It only responded to the probability and uncertainty of Valenced (sweet/bitter) outcomes. This implies the Insula acts as a gatekeeper for decision-making, ensuring that the "Executive" parts of the brain only spend energy on choices that actually have biological consequences.

While traditional models of decision-making emphasize a 'common currency' of subjective value, other studies demonstrate a neural and behavioral dissociation between *informational (generic) and motivational (valenced)* variables. By showing that the mPFC and Occipital cortex track probability even for neutral outcomes, the study suggests that the brain’s 'prediction engine' operates independently of the 'valuation engine,' providing a more robust framework for how humans model complex, uncertain environments.
